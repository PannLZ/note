1. Introduction
The stock market is a complex and dynamic environment, and investors seek to make informed decisions by predicting stock
trends. Accurate stock trend prediction is crucial for investment strategies and risk management [1]. Traditional stock trend prediction
methods often rely on various techniques such as technical analysis, fundamental analysis, and time series analysis. However, these
methods often struggle to capture the intricate relationships and dynamic characteristics of stock market data. Many existing studies
[2,3] assume that prices of different stocks are independent of each other and build prediction models based solely on time series of
information related to each stock, such as historical prices and trading volume (e.g., opening price, closing price, highest price, and
volume).
In practice, the price trends of different stocks are often highly correlated with each other, and these stocks are associated with
common concepts. For example, over the last year, with the popularity of ChatGPT, stocks related to the “GPT concept” in the A-share
market have continued to rise. Companies listed this year that are associated with the ChatGPT concept have seen significant increases

stocks as nodes in a graph and model the relationships between them as edges, allowing these models to leverage rich information
embedded in the network structure. The core idea of GNNs is to learn node representations through interactions among nodes.
They capture the relationships and global structure among nodes by performing information propagation and aggregation in the
graph. GNNs typically employ an iterative approach where each iteration updates the representations of the nodes, enabling them to
gradually focus on the information from their neighboring nodes. This concept is often referred to as neighborhood aggregation.
However, these methods often directly apply GNNs to the financial market, limiting the expressive power of GNNs and failing to
fully harness the value of stock concepts. Through our research analysis and experiments, we have found that over-smoothing [11,12]
and over-compression [13] constrain the performance of GNNs in the application to the stock market. Over-smoothing occurs when,
after several layers, all node representations converge to a constant value, while over-compression happens when information from
distant nodes fails to effectively propagate to certain “bottleneck” areas in the graph, as excessive information gets compressed into
a fixed-length vector. Therefore, designing new architectures that go beyond neighborhood aggregation is crucial for more effective
application of GNNs in financial markets.
In addition, most previous studies assumed that these connections of known concepts in GNNs are static, and therefore information
propagates through these connections according to the same pattern. However, in real financial markets, a stock may have dynamic
correlations with various concepts. For example, Jiuan Medical∗ has two prior concepts: “medical devices,” which it shares with
Yingke Medical∗, and “antigen testing”, which it shares with Shuoshi Biology∗ (∗ these are all stocks listed on the A-share market).
Clearly, during the lockdown period caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, the upward trend in Jiuan Medical’s stock price was primarily
driven by its association with the surging “antigen testing” concept rather than the “medical devices” concept. Therefore, how to
construct a dynamic concept representation network is also key for GNN to predict stock price trends.
In this paper, we propose a new framework called Transformer Graph Network for Stock Trend Forecasting (TGNS) to address the
GNN limitations described above and enhance the model’s representational capabilities in terms of leveraging stock concepts. Un-
like prior GNN-Transformer hybrids, TGNS uniquely integrates Concept Graph Attention (CGA) and Stock Graph Attention (SGA) to
model dynamic concept-stock correlations. This resolves over-smoothing via residual structures while prioritizing high-return stocks
through our novel Weighted Robust Loss (WRL). TGNS leverages the advantages of Transformers in capturing global dependencies
and modeling long-range interactions, complementing the local neighborhood information captured by GNNs. By introducing Trans-
formers, GNNs can integrate global information by considering attention over the entire graph, rather than being limited to local
neighborhoods. This allows GNNs to leverage global structural patterns and dependencies, improving representation learning. TGNS
introduces a more flexible way of modeling relationships between nodes through self-attention mechanisms, enabling GNN to dy-
namically capture complex dependencies and interactions among nodes, surpassing the limitations of traditional message-passing
schemes.
Specifically, TGNS contains a concept graph attention (CGA) module and a stock graph attention (SGA) module that captures
pairwise interactions between all stocks and establishes connections between concept information and stocks. Thus, TGNS can effec-
tively learn both local and global trading information while alleviating the over-smoothing phenomenon. CGA module focuses on
capturing relationships and interactions among different concepts or factors related to stocks. It uses attention mechanisms to assign
weights to the connections between concepts, enabling the model to selectively emphasize or de-emphasize certain concepts based on
their relevance to the task at hand. By incorporating the CGA module, TGNS can effectively leverage the interplay between various
concepts and their impact on stock behavior. Another important module, SGA, can dynamically capture the complex dependencies
and interactions among stocks, which computes attention weights between any two stocks, enabling the learning of mutual influences
between them. Additionally, it can uncover hidden information that cannot be explained by factor correlations in the original data.
To better optimize our TGNS framework, we propose a new loss function called weighted robust loss (WRL). The main characteristic
of the weighted robust loss function is its robustness to outliers. When the difference between predicted and true values is small, WRL
function behaves similarly to the mean squared error (MSE) loss function and is not sensitive to outliers. However, when the difference
is large, the growth rate of the loss function slows down, resulting in a smaller impact from outliers. By increasing the weight values
in the loss function, we can enhance the weights of stocks with higher cross-sectional returns and reduce the weights of stocks with
lower cross-sectional returns, thereby improving the long side performance of the model.
To evaluate the effectiveness of our framework, we conducted extensive experiments on real financial datasets. We compared the
predictive performance of the proposed TGNS with various different methods, including graph-based and attention-based baselines.
The experimental results demonstrate that TGNS performs remarkably well in stock movement prediction. Compared to traditional
GNN methods, TGNS can capture complex dependencies and long-range interactions among stocks more accurately, thus improving
prediction accuracy. Additionally, ablation studies validate the effectiveness of the individual TGNS modules.

2. Related work
In this section, we briefly review recent works on GNNs and Transformers for stock move prediction.
2.1. Graph neural networks (GNNs)
For learning representations of graph-structured data, graph neural networks (GNNs) [14,15] have attracted considerable atten-
tion. GNNs operate on data structured as a graph, which consists of nodes (vertices) and edges (connections between nodes). Nodes
can represent entities, such as users or products, while edges represent relationships or interactions between entities. They allow
for efficient modeling of relationships and interactions between elements within a graph, making them well-suited for various tasks
including node classification, link prediction, and graph classification. The first step in GNNs is to assign an initial embedding or
feature representation to each node in the graph. These embeddings capture the characteristics or attributes of the nodes and serve
as the input to the GNN model. The key concept in GNNs is message passing, where nodes exchange information with their neigh-
bors iteratively [16]. This allows nodes to gather and propagate information throughout the graph, capturing both local and global
dependencies. The GNN layer is employed to integrate the representations and graph structure, enhancing the Transformer layer’s
utilization of the graph structure. Additionally, the GNN layer efficiently captures the information of relevant nodes in the neighbor-
hood through the message passing process [17]. Taking node 𝜐 𝑖 as an example, the message passing process of the GNN layer can be
described as follows:

GNNs have demonstrated remarkable performance in a wide range of application [18]. They have been successfully applied to
social network analysis, recommendation systems, molecular chemistry, computer vision, and natural language processing, among

concept-oriented shared information to enhance the accuracy of stock trend predictions. By leveraging the shared information among
stocks and incorporating individual stock information, HIST achieves improved stock trend forecasting performance. MGRA [20],

Although GNNs have achieved notable success, they encounter challenges like over-smoothing and limited expressiveness. Over-
smoothing occurs when repeated neighborhood aggregation leads to the loss of discriminative information. Limited expressiveness
refers to the difficulty in capturing higher-order dependencies and long-range relationships.
2.2. Transformer
The Transformer architecture, originally introduced in machine translation, is rooted in the idea of attention mechanisms [21,22].
Attention allows the model to focus on different parts of the input sequence while processing each position. The attention mechanism
in Transformers is often referred to as self-attention or scaled dot-product attention. The attention mechanism involves three concepts:
query, key, and value. These are linearly transformed representations of the input sequence that capture different aspects of the
information. The query represents the position in the sequence that we are currently focusing on, while the keys and values represent
all other positions. To determine the importance or relevance of each position to the current query, the dot product similarity between
the query and the keys is computed. This results in a set of similarity scores, where each score represents the similarity between the
query and a particular position in the sequence. The scores quantify the attention or importance that each position receives from
the current query. The similarity scores are then scaled using a softmax function to obtain attention weights. The attention weights
determine how much focus or weight each position should have in the context of the current query. The weighted sum aggregates
the values according to their corresponding attention weights. This produces a context vector or attention output that captures the
relevant information from the entire sequence, weighted by their importance. In the Transformer encoder, each layer computes the
attention distribution for every token and aggregates information from other tokens based on their attention scores. This mechanism
enables each token to gather insights from all other tokens, facilitating the modeling of long-term dependencies in sequential data. The
Transformer’s exceptional effectiveness in representation learning quickly surpassed Recurrent Neural Networks and convolutional
neural networks, establishing itself as the new state-of-the-art model across various tasks [23–26]. Recently, emerging research
streams [26–28] have focused on adapting Transformer architectures to graph-structured data, commonly termed graph Transformers.
A significant research direction within these approaches involves developing specialized mechanisms that enable transformers to
effectively capture graph structural information.

Dwivedi et al. [21] and Kreuzer et al. [29] make use of the eigenvectors derived from the graph Laplacian matrix as a component

Recent breakthroughs in stock trend forecasting have shifted toward Transformer-based architectures and federated learning
to address dynamic market dependencies and non-IID data challenges. Nie et al. [32] introduces a patch-based Transformer that
partitions stock price time series into local segments to capture multi-scale patterns. While effective for high-frequency data, its static
patch structure struggles to adapt to abrupt concept shifts, such as the rapid emergence of AI-driven stock clusters in 2023. Zhou et al.
[33] enhances long-range dependency modeling via adaptive sparse attention but remains limited to sequential analysis, neglecting
cross-sectional concept-stock correlations. These methods highlight the need for frameworks that dynamically integrate concept-level
semantics with fine-grained stock interactions—a gap addressed by TGNS through its dual CGA-SGA architecture (Section 3).

Transformer Encoder: This module is responsible for expanding the model’s receptive field and efficiently aggregating attention
sampling information. It starts by passing the input data through a convolutional layer, which transforms the feature vectors to

Concept Graph Attention (CGA) module: The data processed by Transformer encoder is passed to the Concept Graph Attention
module. In this module, the encoded features are then fed into a masked self-attention layer, allowing the model to learn the returns
that explain the interconnections and correlations between various concepts in the stock market, such as industry or sector rela-
tionships. The output obtained after the calculation of the masked self-attention layer is then subjected to residual processing with
the output of the Graph Transformer module. CGA aims to extract the information that cannot be explained by sector or industry
correlations, representing the residual information from the original data. CGA’s sector/industry-based graph construction (Equation
(6)) and residual learning (Equation (9)) disentangle concept-driven returns without explicit knowledge graphs. The ELU activation
(Equation (7)) enriches nonlinear interactions, outperforming ReLU (Table 4).
Stock Graph Attention (SGA) module: After obtaining the residual hidden state output from CGA module, it is used as input
to the Stock Graph Attention module, where the attention scores between any two stocks are computed. This module employs a
global self-attention layer to learn the returns that elucidate the factor correlations between stocks. It leverages a simple yet effective
algorithm to extract hidden concepts and their corresponding representations. The residual representation from the previous module
is combined with the factor itself to explain specific returns, incorporating both local and global information.
Weighted Robust Loss: The Weighted Robust Loss is a loss function utilized for model optimization. Its purpose is to enhance
the model’s resilience to outliers present in the training data. This loss function assigns weights to different samples, dynamically
adjusting the significance of each sample during training. By doing so, it mitigates the impact of outliers on the model’s performance
and enhances its stability.
Overall, the TGNS model combines the power of Transformer-based encoders, attention mechanisms, and graph neural networks
to capture both local and global dependencies in stock data. The Weighted Robust Loss helps the model handle outliers effectively
during training, leading to improved prediction performance.

3.4. Stock graph attention (SGA) module
The stock graph attention module mainly consists of global self-attention, residual network, and output layer. The difference
between stock graph attention module and the previously mentioned concept graph attention module lies in the following: concept
graph attention module calculates attention weights only between stocks belonging to the same sector or industry, whereas stock
graph attention module calculates attention weights between any two stocks. The purpose of the global self-attention layer is to learn
the mutual influence between any pair of stocks. The stock graph attention module, as described, incorporates global self-attention, a
residual network, and an output layer. Its main distinction from the concept graph attention module lies in the calculation of attention
weights. In the concept graph attention module, attention weights are computed only between stocks that belong to the same sector
or industry. Overall, the stock graph attention module extends the concept graph attention module by considering attention weights
between any two stocks and incorporating a global self-attention layer to capture the mutual influence between stocks. The Stock
Graph Attention (SGA) module in TGNS dynamically captures complex dependencies among stocks by adaptively recalibrating edge
weights based on both historical price patterns and cross-sectional fundamental correlations. Specifically, SGA employs a multi-head
self-attention mechanism to compute pairwise affinity scores between stocks, which are then fused with the predefined industry-sector
graph to generate context-aware adjacency matrices (Eq. (11)). This hybrid approach enables the model to discern transient market
sentiment-driven linkages (e.g., momentum spillovers) alongside structural sector-based connections, addressing the rigidity of static
graph topologies in conventional GNNs. Additionally, the residual architecture enables self-attention heads to dynamically recalibrate
their focus across market regimes. For instance, during sector-agnostic risk events, the residual pathways allow attention heads to
amplify ESG-factor dependencies while suppressing transient sector correlations, a capability absent in static graph constructions. By
iteratively blending residual features (localized pairwise dependencies) with self-attention outputs (global context-aware influences),
the SGA module constructs hierarchical representations. This fusion reveals latent transmission pathways that manifest through
indirect, nonlinear channels undetectable via linear correlation metrics. Similar to the masked self-attention in the concept graph
attention module, we first calculate the attention scores 𝑒1 𝑖𝑗 between any two stocks 𝑖 and 𝑗 based on the hidden state ℎ1 𝑖 .

3.5. Weighted robust loss

than 𝛿, the WRL behaves quadratically similar to the MSE loss. This property makes the WRL less sensitive to small deviations and
noise in the data, similar to the MSE loss. However, if the difference exceeds the threshold 𝛿, the WRL behaves linearly, similar to
the absolute error loss. This characteristic enables the WRL to be more resistant to the influence of outliers, as it limits the impact
of large deviations on the overall loss. By being less influenced by outliers, it allows the model to focus on the underlying patterns
in the data. When training a model with the WRL, the impact of noisy or outlier-laden data points is reduced, enabling the model
to learn more accurate representations of the true underlying distribution. This denoising effect helps the model make more reliable
predictions and enhance its generalization ability on unseen data.

4.2. Implementation
In the CSI 300 stock dataset, we can use the Granger causality test to measure the causal relationship between different stocks. This
method helps determine whether the past returns of one stock can explain the future returns of another, thereby assessing the causal
influence between them. The Appendix A.1 shows some results of Granger causality tests [44]conducted between pairs of stocks in
the CSI 300 dataset. Granger causality tests confirm dynamic interdependencies (e.g., Ping An Bank→China Merchants Bank, p =
0.005), validating our assumption of non-static concept-stock relationships. The Alpha360 model utilizes stock features with a fixed
dimension of 360 (l=360). In our TGNS framework and comparative baseline models, we process all stocks available on each trading
date as a single batch, making the batch size equivalent to the daily stock count in our dataset. Besides, to optimize performance, we
employed a grid search strategy to select the best hyperparameters for our framework and the baseline models, with decisions based
on validation set performance. We search the number of hidden units d in the transformer-based stock feature encoder of our model,
as well as the hidden unit counts in other baselines in {32, 64, 128, 256, 512}; the number of layers in our transformer encoder
and other baselines in {1, 2, 3, 4}; the learning rate in {0.001, 0.0005, 0.0002, 0.0001}.Other baselines were initialized based on
the recommended optimal hyperparameters provided in their original paper. Perform the hyperparameter search by exhaustively
evaluating all combinations of hyperparameters. For each combination, train and evaluate the model using the validation set. Assess
the performance of each model configuration using the defined evaluation metric(s) for example recall. Compare the results obtained
from different hyperparameter combinations. Identify the optimal hyperparameters based on the performance of the models on
the validation set. Table A.2 in Appendix A.2 shows the selection of the number of units and layers in our TGNS framework and
other baselines, and the best learning rate in our framework and other baseline is 0.0002. In the concept graph attention and stock
graph attention modules, the network hidden units are set to 64. Each trading day’s entire set of stocks is treated as a batch, and
the early stopping count is set to 20. The model was trained for 50 epochs. For all experimental runs, the Adam optimizer was
employed, selected for its rapid convergence capabilities. Ultimately, a learning rate of 0.0001 was found to consistently yield the
optimal convergence performance across all experiments. We implement our framework with the PyTorch library [45], and run all
experiments on 4 NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPUs. NVIDIA is one of the major GPU manufacturers, and its official documentation provides
detailed information about the impact of different GPU configurations on performance and applications. Relevant documents and
whitepapers can be found on the NVIDIA Developer website (https://developer.nvidia.com/).

Evaluation Metrics: in the conducted experiments, we employed IC (Information Coefficient), Rank IC, and Precision as evaluation
metrics to assess the performance of stock price prediction. The IC factor is utilized as an indicator of stock selection ability, with
a higher value indicating stronger performance. The maximum IC value is 1, which signifies 100% accuracy in stock selection,
corresponding to the stock with the highest ranking score. Selecting this stock would result in the maximum increase during the
subsequent adjustment cycle. Conversely, an IC value of -1 suggests the largest decline in the next adjustment cycle, representing a
completely inverse indicator. An IC value of 0 or close to 0 indicates a lack of predictive power for the stock, rendering it ineffective. A
high positive IC value implies a higher correlation and stronger influence on the stock price, while a high negative IC value indicates
a stronger inverse correlation. The Rank IC, denoted as

6. Conclusion
This paper presents the Transformer Graph Network for Stock Trend Forecasting (TGNS), a novel framework that integrates Graph
Neural Networks (GNNs) with Transformers to address limitations in traditional stock prediction. By leveraging dynamic concept-stock
relationships, TGNS introduces the Concept Graph Attention (CGA) and Stock Graph Attention (SGA) modules to capture both local
concept interactions and global stock dependencies. This dual-attention design overcomes the over-smoothing issue in conventional
GNNs and enables adaptive modeling of time-varying market dynamics, such as sector rotations driven by emerging concepts (e.g., AI
or pandemic-related industries). The Weighted Robust Loss (WRL) function further enhances TGNS by prioritizing high-return stocks,
improving the effectiveness of the model in long-short strategies. Extensive experiments on the CSI100 / 300 and NASDAQ-100 data
sets validate that TGNS outperforms state-of-the-art methods (e.g. HIST, PatchTST) by 12.3-21. 9% in the Information Coefficient (IC),
demonstrating its superiority in capturing complex dependencies and long-range interactions. Ablation studies confirm the critical
role of CGA and SGA in disentangling concept-driven and factor-based returns. In summary, TGNS advances stock trend forecasting by:
(1) unifying GNNs and Transformers to model dynamic concept-stock correlations, (2) introducing a novel loss function aligned with
investment objectives, and (3) enabling cross-market generalization. These contributions provide a robust foundation for addressing
non-IID financial data challenges and inform future research on integrating macroeconomic indicators or real-time market signals.

draw the following analysis: The Diebold-Mariano test statistic is 0.3015. This value represents the difference between the mean
prediction errors of the two models relative to the standard deviation of their differences. In this case, the positive value indicates
that the mean prediction error of the first TGNS model is slightly higher than that of the second HIST model. The two-tailed p-value
is 0.7630. This value represents the probability of observing the Diebold-Mariano test statistic or a more extreme result under the
null hypothesis, which assumes that the mean prediction errors of the two models are equal. Since the p-value is high, we fail to
reject the null hypothesis and cannot conclude that there is a significant difference in the mean prediction errors between the two
models. The p-value for TGNS model is 0.0260, while the p-value for HIST model is 0.3158. These p-values indicate the probability of
autocorrelation in the corresponding model’s prediction errors. The lower p-value (model TGNS) suggests that the prediction errors
of this model may exhibit autocorrelation, while the higher p-value (model HIST) implies that the prediction errors of this model
may not have autocorrelation. In conclusion, based on the results of the Diebold-Mariano test, we cannot conclude that there is a
significant difference in the predictive ability between these two models. Additionally, the prediction errors of model TGNS may
